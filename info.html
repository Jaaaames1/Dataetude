The 12 Keys

Western music is built on 12 notes, each a semitone apart. Every scale, chord and arpeggio in Excel at Scales can be played starting from any of these 12 root notes.

Some notes have two names — for example C♯ and D♭ refer to the same piano key. The app shows both where relevant.

The white keys on a piano spell out C D E F G A B — the C Major scale. The black keys are the sharps and flats in between.

Major Scale

The major scale is the most common scale in Western music. It has a bright, happy, resolved character. When people hum a tune without thinking about it, it's usually major.

It is built from 7 notes following a specific pattern of tones (T) and semitones (S):

TTS TTT S

In C Major this gives: C D E F G A B C

Twinkle Twinkle Little Star, Happy Birthday and the opening of Eye of the Tiger are all major scales at heart.

Natural Minor Scale

The natural minor scale has a darker, more melancholic or emotional character than major. Much of classical music, rock, and film scores use minor keys for tension, drama or sadness.

It follows this pattern:

TST TST T

In A Natural Minor: A B C D E F G A

The key difference from major is the flattened 3rd, 6th and 7th degrees — these three notes give minor its characteristic dark sound.

A Natural Minor uses exactly the same notes as C Major — they are relative keys. The root note is what changes the feel.

Harmonic Minor Scale

The harmonic minor is a natural minor scale with one change — the 7th degree is raised by a semitone. This creates a stronger pull back to the tonic (the leading tone effect) which is useful when writing chord progressions and harmonies.

TST TST+S S

In A Harmonic Minor: A B C D E F G♯ A

Notice the gap of 3 semitones between the 6th and 7th degrees (F to G♯). This augmented second gives harmonic minor its distinctive exotic, Eastern European or Middle Eastern flavour.

Harmonic minor is widely used in classical music and metal — the raised 7th creates a powerful dominant V chord that pulls strongly back to the tonic.

Melodic Minor Scale

The melodic minor scale resolves an awkward issue in harmonic minor — the large jump between the 6th and 7th. It raises both the 6th and 7th degrees when ascending, making the melody smoother. Traditionally the descending version reverts to natural minor.

Ascending

TST TTT S

A Melodic Minor ascending: A B C D E F♯ G♯ A

Descending

A Melodic Minor descending: A G F E D C B A (same as natural minor, reversed)

In the app you can toggle between ASC and DESC views to see and practise each direction's fingering separately.

Jazz musicians often use melodic minor ascending in both directions — the smooth ascending form works well over many chord types.

Arpeggios

An arpeggio is a chord played one note at a time rather than simultaneously — the notes of a chord spread out into a sequence. The word comes from the Italian arpeggiare, meaning to play the harp.

Where a scale steps through every note in a key, an arpeggio skips through just the chord tones — the 1st, 3rd and 5th degrees (and optionally the 7th).

Major Arpeggio

Built from the 1st, 3rd and 5th of the major scale. In C: C E G C E G (over two octaves)

RootMajor 3rdPerfect 5th

Minor Arpeggio

Built from the 1st, flattened 3rd and 5th. In A: A C E A C E (over two octaves)

RootMinor 3rdPerfect 5th

How Arpeggios Differ from Scales

Arpeggios are essential technique — they train your hand to span wider intervals cleanly and help you hear the outline of chords as you play.

Relative & Parallel Keys

Relative Keys

Every major key has a relative minor — a minor key that shares exactly the same notes, just starting from a different root. The relative minor starts on the 6th degree of the major scale.

MajorRelative Minor
C MajorA Natural Minor
G MajorE Natural Minor
F MajorD Natural Minor
D MajorB Natural Minor
B♭ MajorG Natural Minor

Because they share notes, moving between relative keys is smooth — the same fingering pattern applies, just starting from a different point.

Parallel Keys

Parallel keys share the same root note but use different scales. C Major and C Minor are parallel — they both start on C but have different notes and a very different feel.

Switching between parallel keys is a powerful compositional device — the same root but a sudden shift from bright to dark (or vice versa) creates strong contrast.

In the app, the Relative button swaps between a major key and its relative minor. The Parallel button swaps between major and minor versions of the same root note.

Chords & Progressions

A chord is three or more notes played together. The most common chords in the app are triads — built from three notes stacked in thirds.

Why Fingering Matters

Using the correct fingering makes scales and arpeggios flow smoothly at speed. The key technique is the thumb crossing — passing the thumb under the hand (or the hand over the thumb) to continue the scale without a break in sound.

Standard fingering places thumb crossings at musically logical points and avoids putting the thumb on black keys where possible, which makes the hand position more stable.

Right Hand vs Left Hand

Right and left hand fingerings are often mirror images of each other but not always — the hands naturally sit differently on the keyboard and thumb crossings fall at different points to keep both hands comfortable and independent.

The fingering shown in the app is the standard Royal College of Music recommended fingering. You can customise it in the Fingering Editor to suit your own hand size and technique.
